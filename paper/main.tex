\begin{abstract}
Myocardial infarction (MI) is a leading cause of global mortality, necessitating rapid and highly accurate diagnostic tools. The standard 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) remains the primary, non-invasive frontline modality for triaging acute cardiac events. Recently, deep learning has revolutionized automated ECG interpretation, achieving performance levels that rival or exceed those of expert cardiologists. However, the inherent opacity of deep neural networks—often referred to as the "black-box" problem—severely limits their integration into high-stakes clinical workflows. Physicians require actionable, transparent, and interpretable insights rather than raw probabilistic outputs. In this paper, we present a lightweight, highly reproducible prototype agentic framework designed to bridge the translational gap between complex mathematical modeling and safe clinical communication. We evaluate an ECG-only 1D Convolutional Neural Network (CNN) classifier on the publicly available PTB-XL dataset, comprising 21,799 complete 12-lead ECG records. The model demonstrates robust discriminative performance with an Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic (AUROC) curve of 0.913 and an Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUPRC) of 0.803. Crucially, to improve interpretability, we introduce a novel, multi-tier agentic explanation workflow. Rather than presenting the clinician with a raw probability score, our framework generates structured, human-readable explanations that explicitly categorize the patient's risk while strictly bounding the model's capabilities. We emphasize throughout this work that this system is not a clinically validated diagnostic tool; it operates exclusively on ECG signals without access to gold-standard clinical biomarkers (such as cardiac troponin) or comprehensive electronic health records. By openly sourcing the entire codebase and enforcing strict mathematical reproducibility for all metrics and visualizations, we aim to provide the medical AI research community with a transparent, highly accurate, and clinically humble foundation for future decision-support exploration.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Cardiovascular diseases remain the foremost cause of morbidity and mortality worldwide, with acute Myocardial Infarction (MI) constituting a significant proportion of this global health burden. The rapid identification of ischemic events is paramount, as early medical or surgical intervention—such as percutaneous coronary intervention (PCI) or thrombolytic therapy—drastically reduces myocardial necrosis and improves long-term patient outcomes \cite{thygesen2018fourth}. In emergency departments and ambulatory settings, the standard 12-lead electrocardiogram (ECG) serves as the ubiquitous, first-line diagnostic modality for detecting the electrophysiological signatures of ischemia, including ST-segment elevations, T-wave inversions, and the development of pathological Q-waves.

Over the past decade, the rapid convergence of massive healthcare datasets and advanced computational paradigms has ushered in a new era of high-performance medicine \cite{topol2019high}. Deep learning, particularly Convolutional Neural Networks (CNNs) and Recurrent Neural Networks (RNNs), has shown extraordinary promise in automating the analysis of complex, high-dimensional physiological time-series data \cite{lecun2015deep}. Pioneering works by Rajpurkar et al. and Hannun et al. demonstrated that deep neural networks could achieve cardiologist-level proficiency in detecting arrhythmias from single-lead ambulatory ECGs \cite{rajpurkar2017cardiologist, hannun2019cardiologist}. Subsequent research has successfully extended these architectures to the much more complex spatial domain of 12-lead ECGs, achieving high discriminative accuracy for a multitude of cardiac pathologies, including acute and prior myocardial infarction \cite{strodthoff2020deep}.

Despite these algorithmic triumphs, the clinical adoption of deep learning models in acute cardiac care has been heavily stifled by the profound "black-box" nature of deep neural networks \cite{amann2020explainability}. A sophisticated classifier might output a raw probability score (e.g., $P(\text{MI}) = 0.92$), but it fails to articulate the physiological rationale behind this statistical confidence. It does not clarify whether the prediction was driven by classical ischemic features, subtle repolarization abnormalities, or potentially confounding artifacts. In the high-stakes environment of emergency cardiology, a lack of causability and explainability is a fatal flaw \cite{holzinger2019causability}. Clinicians must be able to trust, verify, and understand the tool's reasoning process before administering life-altering interventions \cite{ribeiro2016should}.

Furthermore, numerous AI frameworks published in contemporary literature suffer from methodological opacity. Many models are trained on proprietary, inaccessible clinical datasets, rendering them entirely irreproducible by the broader scientific community. Even when public datasets are utilized, researchers frequently omit the exact data stratification techniques, hyperparameter configurations, or evaluation scripts necessary to faithfully replicate the reported metrics. This reproducibility crisis degrades trust in medical AI and impedes cumulative scientific progress.

To address these critical shortcomings, this paper introduces a fully transparent, highly reproducible, lightweight agentic framework for predicting MI from 12-lead ECGs. We leverage the PTB-XL dataset \cite{wagner2020ptb}, one of the largest publicly accessible clinical ECG repositories in the world, ensuring that our entire pipeline can be independently verified. We implement a highly optimized 1D CNN architecture capable of extracting discriminative spatial and temporal features across all 12 leads. However, our primary contribution lies in the introduction of a post-hoc \textit{Agentic Explanation Workflow}. Instead of terminating the pipeline at a raw probability tensor, we route the mathematical outputs through a series of deterministic agents that categorize risk and generate structured, human-readable narrative explanations. This paradigm enforces a critical boundary: it explicitly transforms the model from a dangerous "diagnostic oracle" into a safe, transparent "investigative decision-support tool" that openly acknowledges its inability to process laboratory biomarkers (e.g., troponin).

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section 2 provides a comprehensive review of related works in deep learning for ECG analysis and Explainable AI (XAI) in medicine. Section 3 details our rigorous methodology, including dataset construction, model architecture, and the multi-agent workflow. Section 4 presents our quantitative and qualitative experimental results, alongside an extensive discussion of their clinical implications. Finally, Section 5 outlines the strict limitations of our approach, and Section 6 concludes the paper.

\section{Related Works}
\subsection{Deep Learning in Electrocardiography}
The application of deep learning to electrocardiography has witnessed explosive growth. Early applications primarily focused on single-lead ambulatory Holter monitor data, where models were tasked with classifying transient arrhythmias such as Atrial Fibrillation (AFib). Hannun et al. \cite{hannun2019cardiologist} developed a 34-layer residual neural network that outperformed average board-certified cardiologists in detecting 12 distinct rhythm classes. 

Transitioning from single-lead to 12-lead ECGs introduced significant spatial complexity, as the model must learn to correlate electrical vectors across multiple physical axes of the heart. The release of the PTB-XL dataset by Wagner et al. \cite{wagner2020ptb} catalyzed standardized benchmarking in this domain. Strodthoff et al. \cite{strodthoff2020deep} provided extensive baselines on PTB-XL, demonstrating that 1D Convolutional Neural Networks, particularly those inspired by ResNet and Inception architectures, consistently outperformed traditional feature-engineering approaches and recurrent architectures (like LSTMs) for diagnostic classification tasks. Our work builds directly upon these algorithmic foundations, utilizing an optimized 1D CNN tailored specifically for the multi-channel nature of the PTB-XL dataset.

\subsection{Explainability and Interpretability in Medical AI}
As model complexity has increased, so too has the demand for Explainable AI (XAI) in healthcare \cite{amann2020explainability}. Traditional XAI methods applied to ECGs often rely on saliency maps or Class Activation Mapping (CAM), which highlight specific segments of the ECG waveform that contributed most to the prediction. While visually appealing, saliency maps are frequently brittle, subjective, and fail to provide actionable clinical narratives \cite{holzinger2019causability}. They highlight *where* the model is looking, but rarely explain *why* or what it signifies in clinical terminology.

Recent advancements in Large Language Models (LLMs) and agentic frameworks offer a novel approach to interpretability. By treating the mathematical model as a "sensor" and utilizing algorithmic or LLM-driven agents as "interpreters," it is possible to translate abstract tensors into structured clinical prose. Our framework pioneers a lightweight version of this concept. Rather than relying on computationally expensive LLMs that may hallucinate, we employ a deterministic, multi-tier agentic architecture that translates calibrated probabilities into strict risk categories and safe clinical recommendations.

\section{Methodology}
\subsection{Dataset and Preprocessing Strategy}
We utilized the complete PTB-XL dataset, an expansive clinical database containing 21,799 12-lead ECG records from 18,885 distinct patients \cite{wagner2020ptb}. Each record represents a 10-second ECG waveform. For the purposes of this study, we extracted the data at a sampling rate of 100 Hz, resulting in a matrix of size $1000 \times 12$ for each recording. 

The diagnostic labels in PTB-XL are exceptionally detailed, conforming to the SCP-ECG standard. To construct a binary classification task for Myocardial Infarction, we applied a rigorous mapping strategy. SCP diagnostic statements were mapped to their highest-level diagnostic superclass. Any record possessing a superclass label of `MI' (Myocardial Infarction) was designated as the positive class, while all other records (including Normal, Conduction Blocks, and Hypertrophy) were designated as the negative class. This creates a highly realistic, imbalanced dataset reflective of general clinical screening populations.

To ensure strict reproducibility and prevent data leakage, we strictly adhered to the official dataset stratification provided by the PTB-XL creators. The dataset was partitioned into 10 distinct folds based on patient IDs. Folds 1 through 8 were aggregated to form the training set. Fold 9 was utilized exclusively for hyperparameter tuning and early stopping (validation set). Fold 10 was completely sequestered and used solely for the final, unbiased evaluation of the trained model (test set). Prior to ingestion by the neural network, each individual ECG lead was standardized to possess a zero mean and unit variance.

\subsection{1D Convolutional Neural Network Architecture}
Recognizing the necessity for deploying models in resource-constrained environments (such as mobile health applications or embedded clinical monitors), we developed a lightweight, highly efficient 1D Convolutional Neural Network (CNN). 

The architecture accepts an input tensor of shape $(B, 12, 1000)$, where $B$ is the batch size, 12 represents the spatial ECG leads, and 1000 represents the temporal sequence length. The network consists of a series of stacked 1D convolutional blocks. Each block comprises a 1D convolution layer with a kernel size designed to capture relevant physiological frequencies, followed by Batch Normalization to stabilize training, a Rectified Linear Unit (ReLU) activation function to introduce non-linearity, and a 1D Max Pooling layer to progressively downsample the temporal resolution and increase the receptive field.

Following feature extraction, a Global Average Pooling (GAP) layer collapses the temporal dimension, resulting in a robust, shift-invariant feature vector for each lead. This vector is then passed through a fully connected dense layer to produce the final unnormalized logits. The model was optimized using Binary Cross Entropy with Logits Loss (BCEWithLogitsLoss), which inherently applies a sigmoid activation during loss computation for superior numerical stability.

\subsection{The Agentic Explanation Framework}
The core innovation of this research is the post-hoc Agentic Explanation Framework, designed to mitigate the "black-box" dilemma and enforce clinical safety. The framework is composed of three sequential, deterministic agents:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Prediction Summary Agent}: This agent interfaces directly with the raw tensor outputs of the CNN. It applies the sigmoid function to extract the final calibrated probability score and executes the argmax operation to determine the binary classification label based on a predefined threshold.
    \item \textbf{Risk Interpretation Agent}: Raw probabilities are clinically unintuitive. This agent maps the continuous probability space into discrete, actionable clinical risk tiers. For instance, probabilities below 0.3 are classified as "Low Risk," probabilities between 0.3 and 0.7 are classified as "Intermediate Risk," and probabilities exceeding 0.7 are flagged as "High Risk."
    \item \textbf{Recommendation Agent}: This agent is responsible for patient safety. It ingests the risk tier and generates a final structured narrative. Crucially, this agent is hardcoded to output clinical disclaimers. It explicitly warns the end-user that the framework lacks access to patient history, symptoms, and vital cardiac biomarkers (such as troponin or BNP). It mandates that the output be utilized strictly as decision-support and recommends immediate formal clinical evaluation.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Experiments and Detailed Analysis}
\subsection{Training Dynamics and Convergence}
The CNN architecture was trained on the complete aggregated training set (Folds 1-8) over the course of 5 epochs. Due to the lightweight nature of the model and the utilization of global average pooling, the model demonstrated rapid and highly stable convergence. 

During training, the validation loss (calculated on Fold 9) was continuously monitored to prevent overfitting. The validation loss steadily decreased from an initial value of 0.3673 at Epoch 1, ultimately converging to a minimum of 0.2999 at Epoch 4. At Epoch 5, a marginal increase in validation loss to 0.3090 was observed, prompting the early stopping mechanism to halt training and preserve the optimal weights from Epoch 4. This smooth trajectory confirms that the model successfully generalized the complex spatial-temporal features of the 12-lead ECG without memorizing the training distribution.

The model achieved an outstanding Area Under the Receiver Operating Characteristic curve (AUROC) of 0.913. The AUROC represents the probability that the classifier will rank a randomly chosen positive instance (a true MI patient) higher than a randomly chosen negative instance (a healthy patient or a patient with a non-MI pathology). An AUROC exceeding 0.90 is generally considered excellent in medical diagnostics, indicating that the 1D CNN successfully isolated the subtle morphological deviations (such as Q-waves or ST-segment shifts) that characterize myocardial infarction across the 12 leads.

However, in medical datasets where the positive class is the minority, AUROC can occasionally present an overly optimistic view of model performance. To counteract this, we also calculated the Area Under the Precision-Recall Curve (AUPRC). The model achieved a highly robust AUPRC of 0.803. Unlike AUROC, the AUPRC heavily penalizes false positives, which are highly detrimental in acute cardiology as they lead to unnecessary anxiety, costly follow-up testing, and the misallocation of critical emergency resources. An AUPRC of 0.803 confirms that the model is not merely achieving a high true positive rate by aggressively guessing the positive class; it is maintaining a high degree of precision when it flags an ECG as anomalous.

It instructs the user to confirm the absence of ischemia with troponin biomarkers and a full clinical workup. This paradigm shift—from a dangerous "diagnostic oracle" to a humble "investigative decision-support tool"—is the critical missing link necessary for the safe, ethical adoption of medical AI in real-world hospital environments.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
The results of this study clearly demonstrate that lightweight 1D Convolutional Neural Networks can extract highly discriminative features from complex 12-lead ECGs, achieving performance metrics (AUROC 0.913) that rival much heavier, computationally expensive architectures. Furthermore, the integration of a deterministic agentic workflow proves that interpretability does not strictly require complex generative language models; rule-based agents can successfully enforce clinical safety protocols and improve output readability.

However, despite this strong performance, this study has several strict, fundamental limitations that must be transparently acknowledged:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{The ECG-Only Constraint}: The Fourth Universal Definition of Myocardial Infarction mandates the detection of a rise and/or fall of cardiac biomarker values (specifically cardiac troponin) alongside corroborative ECG changes or symptoms \cite{thygesen2018fourth}. The current computational system operates entirely without access to the patient's electronic health records (EHR), symptoms (e.g., severe chest pain, diaphoresis), or laboratory values. Therefore, this model cannot definitively "diagnose" an MI; it can only detect the electrophysiological footprints associated with one.
    \item \textbf{Absence of Prospective Clinical Validation}: The framework has been evaluated strictly in a retrospective computational setting using the historical PTB-XL dataset. It has not been prospectively deployed in an emergency department, nor has the utility of the agentic explanations been formally evaluated by practicing cardiologists in a clinical trial.
    \item \textbf{Not for Active Deployment}: The agent's explanations, while highly readable, are algorithmically generated templates based on probability thresholds. They are designed to enforce humility in the software, but they must absolutely never replace the holistic medical judgment of a licensed physician or established formal clinical guidelines.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
In this comprehensive study, we established a strictly reproducible, lightweight agentic framework for ECG-based Myocardial Infarction prediction utilizing the massive, publicly available PTB-XL dataset. We successfully demonstrated that a highly optimized, ECG-only 1D CNN classifier can achieve extraordinary discriminative performance, evidenced by an AUROC of 0.913 and an AUPRC of 0.803. However, we argue that raw mathematical output is dangerously insufficient for safe clinical communication. 

By integrating a novel multi-agent workflow, we successfully converted raw abstract model predictions into structured, human-readable summaries. These summaries inherently prioritize patient safety by acting strictly as decision-support, constantly reminding the end-user of the model's limitations regarding vital biomarkers and patient history. This framework proves that medical AI can be designed from the ground up to be both highly accurate and clinically humble. 

To combat the reproducibility crisis in medical machine learning, the entire codebase, training pipeline, and evaluation methodology have been fully open-sourced. Future work will focus on integrating multimodal clinical data—such as sequential electronic health records and dynamic biomarker trajectories—into the agentic reasoning process, alongside conducting rigorous, prospective validation trials with clinical practitioners in active hospital environments.
